Trim Galore! behaviour:

Single-end
The *_trimmed.fq.gz are the final output of single-end trimming.

Paired-end
The *_trimmed.fq.gz are produced as intermediate output 
(as R1 and R2 are trimmed individually in the first instance). 
Once the trimming has completed, Trim Galore will launch a round of 'validation' 
(which is is where the files get the val in their names from), 
which primarily performs length-cutoff filtering (and a few more optional things I believe). 
Once the validation is complete, the trimmed files will be deleted, 
and you are left with only the files N1_1_val_1.fq.gz and N1_2_val_2.fq.gz.


Regarding the adpater content (circa 40%) I found this reddit thread
https://www.reddit.com/r/bioinformatics/comments/iguxlz/regarding_adapter_content/

They say basically not to worry too much about it

The longer your reads, the higher your adapter content. 
As the read length increases toward the greatest length of any insert in your library, 
the proportion of reads with adapter content approaches 100%, 
because 100% of readable library molecules have adapters by definition. 
All this tells you is that your sequence reads were longer than your inserts 
40% of the time. It's up to you to determine whether your inserts were unusually 
short for this protocol or you're wasting money on unnecessarily long reads. 

Not something you need to worry about too much (some madlads don't even trim before alignment anymore...).
Without a tonne more info - Could be machine based (fastq generation can include some adapter trimming) 
or some other processing happening between the basecalls and the FASTQ files that you have. 
Could also be reading all the way through your fragment and into part of the adapter. 
Could even be a part of your sequenced material that looks a bit like an adapter, though this should be rare.
If after trimming it aligns okay for your purposes, I wouldn't worry too much.

In https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12098861/ they say:

We developed an in silico strategy to identify segments of 
reference genomes with inherent low mappability. 
These segments are enriched in repeats and low-complexity 
regions that preclude the unambiguous alignment of short reads 
to unique locations in the genome. Hard to map regions in the reference 
genome is a concern in the S/G1 methods because late replicating regions 
are defined by their lower read coverage relative to G1. It is therefore 
important to exclude low mappability regions from the analysis to avoid 
misclassifying some of them as late replicating. We located these regions 
and created a low mappability drop list for masking the genome during downstream 
analysis 

This study was done on Z.mais, for which blacklisted regions do not exist.
I already removed blacklisted regions, which explicitly take into account low
mappability.

They also did this, might need to do:

o remove spikes in read coverage, which are likely due to collapsed repeats, 
0.25% of 10-kb bins windows with the highest coverage in each G1 sample 
(for EdU-S/G1 and S/G1 independently) were combined into a pooled 
high coverage drop list and removed from all S and G1 samples. 
While spikes in read coverage are rare, they accumulate a disproportionate 
number of reads which distort the 1X normalization step, 
so these regions are removed prior to normalization.

    - span = bins_per_smoothing / bins_per_chromosome
